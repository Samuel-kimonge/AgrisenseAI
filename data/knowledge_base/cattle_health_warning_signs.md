## Know what is normal
Check your animals daily. A healthy adult cow is alert, eats and drinks well, chews the cud regularly, has a shiny coat, moist nose and firm normal dung. Body temperature for adult cattle is typically around 38.0 to 39.3 degrees Celsius. Record normal behaviour and milk yield so you notice change early.

## General warning signs
Standing apart from the herd, dull eyes, drooping ears, reduced appetite, not chewing the cud, a sudden drop in milk yield, weight loss, rough coat, and weakness are general signs that something is wrong.

## Signs by body system
Breathing: fast, laboured or noisy breathing, coughing, nasal discharge. Digestion: diarrhoea, blood in dung, bloating on the left side, constipation, drooling. Skin: lumps, scabs, hair loss, ticks, swellings. Legs and feet: lameness, swollen joints, sores between the hooves. Udder: hard, hot or swollen udder, clots or blood in milk. Eyes: discharge, cloudiness, squinting. Nervous system: staggering, circling, tremors, fits, inability to stand. Reproduction: abortion, retained placenta, abnormal discharge.

## Signs that need urgent help
Call a veterinarian immediately for an animal that cannot stand, is bloated and struggling to breathe, has fits or staggering, is bleeding from body openings, has very high or very low temperature, or when animals die suddenly. Do not open the carcass of an animal that died suddenly; contact the veterinary authority.

## Record keeping
Note which animal, what signs, when they started, the temperature, how many animals are affected, recent feed or pasture changes, and any new animals introduced. This information helps the veterinarian.

## Notifiable diseases
Some diseases, such as foot-and-mouth disease, anthrax and lumpy skin disease, must be reported to the veterinary authority in many countries. If you suspect one, isolate the animal and report it promptly.

## Bloat (swollen left side)
Bloat is a build-up of gas in the rumen that shows as a swollen left flank behind the ribs. The animal may look uncomfortable, kick at its belly, stop eating or chewing the cud, and breathe with difficulty. Severe bloat can kill within hours and is an emergency: contact a veterinarian immediately. Bloat is more likely after sudden access to lush legume pasture, wet grass or grain. Keep the animal calm, remove access to the risky feed, and do not give home remedies or oils unless a veterinarian instructs you.
